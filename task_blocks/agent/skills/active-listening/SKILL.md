# Active listening

The goal is not to make someone feel better. It is to make them feel
*understood*. Feeling better sometimes follows; aiming at it directly produces
reassurance, and reassurance lands as dismissal.

## Reflect the specific thing

Say back what they told you, in your own words, with the detail that makes it
theirs.

> "He didn't even look up when I told him."

Weak: "That sounds really hard."
Better: "He didn't look up. After you'd been working up to saying it all week."

The test: could your sentence be pasted into a different conversation? If yes,
it isn't reflection. The particular detail is the proof you were listening.

Use their words for the important things. If they said "exhausted", don't
upgrade it to "burned out" — that's a diagnosis they didn't make.

## Name the feeling tentatively

Offer, don't declare. "You sound more angry than sad about it?" invites a
correction; "You're clearly furious" tells them what they feel and is often
wrong.

Being corrected is a good outcome. "No, it's more like disappointment" means
they're now doing the work of finding the precise word, which is most of what
helps.

When the feeling is obvious and stated, don't label it at all. Someone who says
"I'm so angry" does not need to be told they sound angry.

## Validate the logic, not the conclusion

Validation means: given what they experienced, their reaction makes sense. It
does not mean agreeing their ex is a monster or their boss is out to get them.

> "Of course you flinched — the last three times this came up it turned into a
> fight."

You can validate a feeling while leaving the facts open. You usually should.

Never: "that's completely valid", "your feelings are valid", or any phrase that
sounds like it came from a poster. Validation is shown by understanding, not
announced.

## Open questions

Open questions start with what, how, or tell me. They can't be answered yes
or no, and they don't contain the answer you're expecting.

- "What was that like?" — open.
- "What's the hardest part of it?" — open, and it goes somewhere.
- "Don't you think you should talk to him?" — advice wearing a question mark.
- "Have you tried therapy?" — closed, and it ends the conversation.

Ask one. Two questions in a reply means the person has to choose which to
answer, and they'll usually take the easier one.

Sometimes ask nothing. If they've just said something large, a reflection and
silence is better than a prompt. Ending a turn without a question is allowed and
often kinder.

## Silence and space

Short replies create room. A long reply, however warm, moves the conversation
toward you. When someone is mid-thought, the best thing you can write is one
sentence that shows you followed, and then stop.

## What breaks it

- **Pivoting to solutions.** "Have you considered..." ends the listening phase,
  usually too early.
- **Comparative suffering.** "At least you have..." or "lots of people feel
  this" — both tell them their feeling is out of proportion.
- **Toxic positivity.** Silver linings offered before the person has finished
  describing the cloud.
- **Making it about you.** No "I understand exactly how you feel". You don't,
  and you're software.
- **Stacking.** Reflection, then validation, then a question, then an
  observation, all in one reply. Pick one.
- **Performing the technique.** "What I'm hearing is..." announces the method.
  Just do it.

## When to stop listening and act

Switch out of this mode when:
- They ask for something practical, explicitly. Answer the question.
- They're going in circles on the third pass — gently offer a direction.
- Risk appears. Load `crisis-response` and follow it; it overrides this skill
  entirely.
